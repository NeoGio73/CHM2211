1At the bench

3The spectrometer

This is the spectrometer's software, as on the computer beside the magnet. Lock on the solvent's deuterium, shim until the lock level is as high as you can get it, record your spectra, then process them: Fourier transform, phase, baseline, reference, and pick peaks and integrate. The guide to running the NMR takes you through each step.

Lock off

Turn each shim until the lock level stops rising, then go round again: they affect each other. Z1 and Z2 matter most.

5The IR spectrum

This is the IR's software. Wipe the diamond crystal clean, record a background with nothing on it, then put your unknown on the crystal (part 1, the IR tab) and record its spectrum. Posted beside the instrument

  • Never let a liquid touch the windows of the sample compartment.
  • Keep dichloromethane and other chlorinated solvents away from the IR: their vapour in the hot source turns into hydrogen chloride and phosgene. Run the IR on the pure compound, never on the GC solution.
  • The instrument stays switched on. Switching it off and on every day makes it less stable.

6The mass spectrum

The gas chromatograph separates what was injected; as each compound leaves the column, the mass spectrometer strikes it with electrons (70 eV) and sorts the ions by mass. You run it as an operator does: check that it is ready, check its tune, set the method, set up the run, then inject by hand and press Start on the GC. What you choose changes the chromatogram and the spectrum you get. Then click the top of your compound's peak to see its mass spectrum.

7Your structure

Put it all together: draw the one structure that fits the formula and every spectrum, name it, and say how each kind of spectrum supports it. The page does not check your structure: your instructor marks it.

Carbons are the corners and ends of lines, with their hydrogens left out, as in your textbook. Bond: click to add a bond, or drag from an atom to aim one; click a bond to make it double, then triple. C, N, O…: click an atom to change it. A ring button puts a ring where you click, hangs it from an atom, or builds it onto a bond. Ctrl+Z also undoes.

The name comes from PubChem, the chemistry database of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, and is the name of the structure you have drawn: it does not say whether that structure is your unknown. Your report tells your instructor that you used it.
