1What you will do

You are given an unknown organic compound and its molecular formula. The code at the top of the lab page decides which compound it is, so your classmates' unknowns are different from yours. In the virtual room you will:

  • make up an NMR sample in CDCl₃, put it into the magnet, lock, shim and record ¹H, ¹³C and DEPT-135 spectra, then process them yourself;
  • record an IR spectrum of the pure compound on an ATR crystal;
  • inject a solution into a GC-MS and read the mass spectrum of your compound's peak;
  • enter what you read into tables, which the page checks as you go;
  • draw the one structure that fits everything, name it, and explain how each spectrum supports it. The structure is not checked by the page: your instructor marks it.

The guide to running the NMR takes you through every click at the spectrometer. This review is about the chemistry: what the spectra mean.

2Start with the formula

Before looking at a single spectrum, work out the degrees of unsaturation (also called the index of hydrogen deficiency): how many rings and π bonds the molecule must have. Every ring, every C=C, every C=O counts 1; a triple bond counts 2.

degrees of unsaturation = C − (H + X)/2 + N/2 + 1

C, H, N and X are the numbers of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and halogen (F, Cl, Br, I) atoms. Oxygen and sulfur do not change it. Four or more often means a benzene ring: one ring and three C=C.

3Why each step at the NMR matters

The room works the way a real spectrometer does, so each step has a reason. The guide says how to do each one; here is why.

StepWhy it matters
Dissolve in CDCl₃Deuterium gives no ¹H signal, so the solvent does not bury your compound. Only the trace of CHCl₃ left in it shows, at 7.26 ppm (¹H); its carbon gives a small triplet at 77.16 ppm (¹³C). Water in the solvent shows near 1.56 ppm.
Fill the tube to about 4 cm and set the depthThe coil that sends and picks up the signal sees only the middle of the sample. The liquid must cover it, with its middle at the coil's middle, or the field is uneven and every line broadens.
LockThe spectrometer watches the solvent's deuterium signal and corrects the field so it never drifts. Without a lock, lines smear while scans are added up.
ShimSmall extra coils make the field the same all through the sample. The more even the field, the narrower and taller the lines, and the higher the lock level: that is how you know when you are done.
Scans and receiver gainEach scan is one pulse and one recording, the FID. Adding scans makes the signal grow faster than the noise: four times the scans gives a spectrum twice as clean. ¹³C needs hundreds of scans, since only about 1 carbon in 100 is ¹³C. Too high a gain clips the FID and ruins the spectrum.
Fourier transformTurns the FID, all the frequencies sounding together, into a spectrum of peaks.
Phase and baselineEvery peak must stand upright and symmetric on a flat baseline, or its position and its integral are wrong.
ReferenceChemical shifts are measured from TMS, set at exactly 0.00 ppm. An unreferenced spectrum can be off by 0.1 ppm, which makes every shift you report wrong.

4Reading a ¹H spectrum

A ¹H spectrum answers four questions. Ask them in this order for every spectrum.

  1. How many signals? One for each set of equivalent hydrogens: hydrogens that symmetry or free rotation makes identical. The three H of a CH₃ give one signal; the two CH₃ groups of an isopropyl group give one signal between them.
  2. Where (the chemical shift, δ)? Hydrogens near electronegative atoms, π bonds or rings come further left (downfield). The table below gives the usual places.
  3. How many H (the integral)? The area of a signal is proportional to the number of hydrogens that give it. Integrals give ratios: scale them so they add up to the H in your formula.
  4. How many neighbours (the splitting)? A signal is split by hydrogens on the next carbon(s). With n equivalent neighbours it has n + 1 lines. The gap between the lines is the coupling constant J, in Hz; two groups that split each other share the same J.

The D₂O shake

Hydrogens on O or N trade places with deuterium from D₂O, and deuterium gives no ¹H signal. Shake the sample with a drop of D₂O, record again, and any signal that shrinks or disappears is an OH or NH. A new small peak of HOD appears near 4.7 ppm. In the lab this step is optional.

5¹³C and DEPT-135

A ¹³C spectrum is recorded with the hydrogens decoupled, so every kind of carbon gives one single line. Count the lines: if there are fewer than the carbons in the formula, the molecule has symmetry. Line heights do not count carbons: a carbon with no hydrogens is usually small.

DEPT-135 sorts the carbons by how many hydrogens they carry:

  • CH₃ and CH point up,
  • CH₂ points down,
  • a carbon with no H (C=O, a ring carbon with a substituent, C≡N) does not appear.

Read the two side by side: a line in the ¹³C spectrum that is missing from the DEPT is a carbon with no hydrogen. The solvent's triplet at 77.16 ppm is not in the DEPT either.

6IR: which bonds are there

An IR spectrum shows dips (bands) where bonds absorb infrared light. You will not explain every band: look for the ones that say which groups are present, and for the ones that are missing, which rule groups out. A good order: is there a C=O near 1650–1820? An O–H or N–H above 3200? A C≡N near 2250? Benzene ring bands near 1600 and 1500?

The lab's IR is an ATR instrument: the sample sits on a crystal. Its bands are in the same places as in any IR spectrum. Record a background first, with the crystal clean: without it, the air's carbon dioxide (a doublet near 2350) and water vapour show up.

7The mass spectrum

In a GC-MS the gas chromatograph separates what was injected, and each compound leaves the column at its own retention time. As it comes off, the mass spectrometer strikes the molecules with fast electrons (70 eV). Each molecule loses an electron to become the molecular ion, M⁺, and many molecular ions break into fragments. The spectrum shows the ions by their mass (m/z), the tallest set to 100%.

  • M⁺, the molecular ion, is usually the highest m/z of any size: the molar mass, counted with the most common isotopes. Check it against your formula. It can be small, or even missing when the ion breaks up completely.
  • The base peak is the tallest; it is often a fragment, not M⁺.
  • Odd M⁺, odd number of N (the nitrogen rule): a compound of C, H, O and halogens has an even molar mass.
  • M+1: ¹³C makes a small peak one mass up, about 1.1% of M⁺ for every carbon. C, H and N are "+1" elements: their heavier isotope is one mass up.
  • M+2: chlorine and bromine have two common isotopes, two mass units apart. One Cl gives an M+2 about a third as tall as M⁺; one Br gives an M+2 about as tall as M⁺. Cl, Br, S and O are "+2" elements (in O and S the heavier isotope is rare, so their M+2 is small).
  • Fragments are read as losses from M⁺: the difference in mass says what fell off.

    8Worked from start to finish

    A practice compound of formula C4H9Br. It is not one of the lab's unknowns, and it is the practice sample whose processed spectra appear in the guide. Look at the four spectra, then walk through the reasoning a step at a time.

    1. The formula

      C₄H₉Br: 4 − (9 + 1)/2 + 1 = 0 degrees of unsaturation. No ring, no double bond: an open chain with single bonds only.

    2. The mass spectrum

      Two peaks of equal height at m/z 136 and 138: M⁺ and M+2 of a compound with one Br (⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br). The base peak, m/z 57, is 136 − 79: the molecule has lost its Br and left C₄H₉⁺.

    3. The IR

      Only C–H bands below 3000 (sp³ C–H) and bends near 1460 and 1380. No broad O–H, no C=O near 1700. The strong band near 640 cm⁻¹ is the C–Br stretch.

    4. ¹³C and DEPT-135

      Four lines for four carbons: no symmetry, every carbon different. In the DEPT, three point down (CH₂) and one points up (13.2: CH₃). So the carbons are CH₃, CH₂, CH₂, CH₂: no branch, since a branch would need a CH or a carbon with no H. The CH₂ at 33.4 carries the Br; the one at 34.8 is next to it.

    5. ¹H: four signals, 2 : 2 : 2 : 3

      The integrals add up to 9, the H in the formula. The 2H triplet at 3.41 is a CH₂ on the carbon bearing Br (3.3–4.5), with two neighbours. The 3H triplet at 0.94 is a CH₃ with two neighbours. Between them, the quintet at 1.85 (4 neighbours: 2 + 2) and the sextet at 1.47 (5 neighbours: 2 + 3).

    6. The structure

      Everything fits one straight chain with Br on the end: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br, 1-bromobutane. Check it against every spectrum: four kinds of C, four kinds of H in a 3 : 2 : 2 : 2 ratio, each split by its neighbours, M⁺ 136/138, no O–H, no C=O.

    9Your turn

    A second practice compound, also not one of the lab's unknowns: formula C4H10O. In its ¹H spectrum the broad signal at 2.1 ppm disappeared after a D₂O shake. Answer the questions, one spectrum at a time.

      It is 2-methylpropan-1-ol, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH. No unsaturation; an O–H (broad band near 3335, the signal that vanished with D₂O, the C–O near 1041); three kinds of carbon for four carbons, because the two CH₃ are the same; a 6H doublet (two CH₃ beside one H), a 2H doublet at 3.40 (CH₂ on O beside the same H), and that one H split by all eight neighbours into a nonet near 1.77. In the mass spectrum, M⁺ 74, m/z 56 (M − 18, water lost), 43 (C₃H₇⁺) and 31 (CH₂=OH⁺, from the CH₂OH end).
